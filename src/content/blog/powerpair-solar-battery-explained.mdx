Rooftop solar does not automatically mean a home will have power during a utility outage. Most ordinary grid-tied systems are designed to shut down when the grid fails so they do not energize utility lines unexpectedly.

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that residential solar panels alone generally are not enough for outage resilience. A system needs a properly configured inverter and storage to operate independently; see the DOE overview of [solar and resilience](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/solar-and-resilience-basics).

## What happens to ordinary grid-tied solar

Solar panels produce direct-current electricity. An inverter converts that power into the alternating current used by the home and coordinates with the utility grid.

During a significant grid disruption, a typical grid-following inverter disconnects. This protects workers and equipment from unintended power flowing onto lines that should be de-energized. The panels may be in full sun, but the ordinary grid-tied system still will not supply the house.

## What a battery changes

A compatible solar-plus-storage system can separate from the grid and create a controlled source of power for selected home circuits. This is often called islanded operation.

During an outage, a correctly designed system can:

- disconnect safely from the utility;
- supply approved backup loads from the battery;
- use available solar production to support loads or recharge storage;
- continue serving selected loads after sunset until stored energy is depleted.

Not every solar battery system provides the same outage behavior. The inverter, transfer equipment, electrical design, battery capacity, output rating, selected circuits, and installation all matter.

## Capacity is not the same as power

Two measurements answer different questions:

- **Energy capacity**, usually measured in kilowatt-hours, affects how long stored energy can last.
- **Power output**, usually measured in kilowatts, affects which loads can run at the same time.

Runtime cannot be promised from battery capacity alone. Appliance starting demand, weather, solar production, battery reserve settings, efficiency losses, and household behavior all change the result.

## Questions to ask before buying

- Which circuits will operate during an outage?
- What continuous and starting loads can the system support?
- Can the solar array recharge the battery while islanded?
- What happens when the battery reaches its reserve limit?
- Is the proposed equipment approved for the intended configuration?
- Who is responsible for design, permitting, utility coordination, and service?

Ask for these answers in writing and make sure the proposed system matches the loads you actually consider essential.

## Portable charging and whole-home backup are different

A [portable emergency power bank](/sidekick) can help keep phones and compatible small USB devices charged. It is useful, affordable, and easy to keep close, but it is not home battery storage.

If your priorities include refrigeration, well pumps, HVAC, medical equipment, or multiple home circuits, begin with a [whole-home backup assessment](/home-backup). The goal is to size the conversation around your actual loads, not a generic runtime table.
